Proposition: Women around the world are closing the gender gap.

FOR the Proposition

Worldwide, the college enrollment gap flipped from 1.6 percentage points in men's favor in 1990 to 5.4 points in women's favor in 2019.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Computing and labeling the gender gap in percentage points (Score: +1, somewhat earnest). We calculated the gap by subtracting men's college enrollment rate from women's and labeled it at both ends of the chart: −1.6 percentage points in 1990 and +5.4 in 2019. This makes the argument more persuasive because it gives readers one memorable number showing the gap flipping in women's favor, instead of making them compare two lines on their own. Both endpoints are shown honestly, and we labeled them in percentage points instead of "%" so the change wouldn't be misread as a percent increase.
  • Emphasizing women's line and muting men's line (Score: −1, somewhat deceptive). We made the women's line bold purple and the men's line thin and gray to keep the focus on women's progress. This worked well at making women's growth the main story, but it quietly downplays that men's enrollment also grew a lot, from 14.4% to 36.2%. The data shows both groups rising with women rising faster, while the styling suggests women's increase is the one that matters.
  • Starting the timeline in 1990 (Score: −1, somewhat deceptive). We started the x-axis in 1990, when women were still behind men in college enrollment, so the chart could show a clear flip. That was important for building a narrative of progress, but the start year is a framing choice that shapes how big the change looks. All of the data is real, but the starting point was chosen to set up the story.
  • Annotating the 2002 crossover (Score: −1, somewhat deceptive). We added the annotation "2002: women pass men and never look back" to mark the year women's enrollment passed men's, and shaded the area between the lines after that point. This gives readers a clear turning point, but the gap in 2002 was tiny (21.9% vs 21.4%, only 0.5 points), and "never look back" makes the moment sound more dramatic than it was.
  • Using a line chart that shows every year (Score: +1, somewhat earnest). We plotted women's and men's enrollment for every year from 1990 to 2019. This persuades by letting readers watch women slowly close the gap and pull ahead, which makes the trend feel real and consistent, and nothing is hidden between the endpoints. We originally considered a bar chart with only 1990 and 2019, but chose the line chart because it shows all the data, including that both groups rose over time.

AGAINST the Proposition

In five countries and regions we selected, women's college enrollment rose sharply while their share in the workforce fell, with China showing the largest contrast.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Intentionally selecting five countries and regions (Score: −1, somewhat deceptive). We picked five places where women's college enrollment increased and workforce participation decreased, which makes the argument more persuasive by showing a consistent pattern. This emphasized the contrast well, but it could lead viewers to believe the same trend happens everywhere, when women's workforce participation actually rose in places like Latin America and high-income countries. We considered a broader selection but kept these five and added a subtitle saying they were chosen on purpose.
  • Using green and red for opposing trends (Score: +1, somewhat earnest). We used green for increases in college enrollment and red for decreases in workforce participation to reinforce the contrast between educational progress and work. This worked because it nudges viewers to read education gains as good and workforce declines as bad. We considered more neutral colors, but chose red and green because they made the chart more persuasive while keeping the actual values unchanged.
  • Showing only women instead of comparing to men (Score: −2, deceptive). We showed only women's enrollment and workforce changes to keep the focus on the gap between rising education and falling work. This made the argument clearer, but leaving men out could make viewers think the workforce decline is unique to women. When we checked, men's workforce participation also fell in all five places (for example, from 80.1% to 74.3% worldwide). We kept the women-only comparison, but we recognize it gives an incomplete picture of gender inequality.
  • Highlighting China with an annotation (Score: +2, earnest). We annotated China because it had both the largest increase in women's college enrollment and the largest decrease in women's workforce participation of the five. This persuades by pointing viewers to the clearest example of the contrast without changing any data, though it gives China more attention than the others. We considered relying only on the number labels, but the annotation makes the main takeaway easier to spot.
  • Using a diverging bar chart (Score: +1, somewhat earnest). We used diverging bars so the opposing trends are noticeable right away and easy to compare across places. The tradeoff is that it only shows the change between the start and end years, not how the values moved over time. We considered line charts to show the full trends, but chose diverging bars because they made the contrast between education and work more visually persuasive.

Final Reflection

What surprised us most was that we never had to change a single number to make either chart persuasive. In the college chart, small choices like starting in 1990, making women's line bold purple, and calling 2002 the year women "never look back" did almost all of the work. In the workforce chart, the diverging bars, the green and red colors, and the China annotation turned the same World Bank data into the opposite argument. The hardest part was the "against" side, because the first version we made at the checkpoint mostly relied on a truncated y-axis, and once we looked at it critically, the trick felt too easy to spot. Switching to the change in two different measures side by side was more persuasive and much harder to see through.

Working on the "against" chart is also where the ethics started to feel real. To build a consistent pattern, we had to leave out places where women's workforce participation actually rose, like Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Latin America, and the chart still looked completely honest because every number on it was real. We also found that men's workforce participation fell in all five places we chose, and that part of the drop for women comes from young women staying in school longer. Neither of those facts appears on the chart, and either one would weaken its argument. That made us realize cherry-picking doesn't feel like lying while you're doing it, which is exactly what makes it risky.

We now think of ethical visualization as emphasizing a pattern on purpose while still being honest about how you got there. Persuasion feels acceptable to us when the data is accurate, the selection is disclosed, and a careful reader could still find the full story, like how our college chart still shows men's enrollment rising even though it isn't emphasized. We would draw the line at changing data, hiding context that would reverse the conclusion, or presenting a hand-picked trend as if it were universal. That is why we disclosed the five selected places in the subtitle of the workforce chart, and why we think the women-only comparison is the most deceptive choice in our project.
