Project 2: Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Aarman Sachdev, assachdev@ucsd.edu
Chase Wada, cmwada@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Women around the world are closing the gender gap.

FOR the Proposition

Worldwide, the college enrollment gap flipped from 1.6 percentage points in men's favor in 1990 to 5.4 points in women's favor in 2019.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Computing and Labeling the Gender Gap in Percentage Points (Score: +1, Somewhat Earnest). We made a measurable gap by subtracting men's college enrollment rate from women's and then labeled it at both ends of the chart. It was −1.6 percentage points in 1990 and +5.4 percentage points in 2019. The argument is more persuasive because it gives people a memorable number that shows how the gap flips from men's to women's favor. If it were not for this, people would have to look at and analyze the two separate lines on their own. Both endpoints are shown honestly and they are labeled with the gap in percentage points instead of "%" so it wouldn't be misread, so this is effective.
  • Emphasizing Women's Line and Muting Men's Line (Score: −1, Somewhat Deceptive). We made the women's line a bolded purple color and the men's line was thin and gray to draw more attention and focus to women's progress. This was good at making the women's growth the main focus of the chart, but doing so slightly hides that men's enrollment also grew from 14.4% to 36.2%. The graph and data show that both groups rose but that women rose faster, but the styling suggests women's increase was the only one.
  • Starting the Timeline in 1990 (Score: −1, Somewhat Deceptive). We started the x-axis in 1990 to exaggerate that this is when women were still behind men in terms of college enrollment. This naturally makes the flip in the gap easier to show. This was important in order for us to create a narrative of progress, but since the start year on the x-axis is a framing choice, it shapes how the magnitude of the change looks. All of the data is real, but the starting point was chosen to set up the story.
  • Annotating the 2002 Crossover (Score: −1, Somewhat Deceptive). We added an annotation on the chart that says "2002: women pass men and never look back" to show the year and point when women's enrollment passed men's. This helps show viewers a clear data point where the flip happens, and the slightly shaded area after this point helps exaggerate the difference in female enrollment. The gap in 2002 was small (0.5 percentage points, 21.9% vs 21.4%), and the message about never looking back makes it seem more dramatic than it is.
  • Using a Line Chart That Shows Every Year (Score: +1, Somewhat Earnest). We used a line chart that shows women's and men's college enrollment for every single year from 1990 to 2019. This helps persuade viewers by letting them see how women slowly close the gap and pull ahead, which makes the trend feel real and consistent. This worked well for this visualization because nothing is hidden between the endpoints. Viewers are able to see the full data and how it moves through the years, including how both groups rose over time. We originally considered a bar chart with just the endpoints of 1990 and 2019, but ultimately chose the line chart because it shows all the data and is easier to visualize and analyze.

AGAINST the Proposition

In five countries and regions we selected, women's college enrollment rose sharply while their share in the workforce fell, with China showing the biggest contrast.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Intentionally Selecting Five Countries and Regions (Score: −1, Somewhat Deceptive). We intentionally selected five regions where female college enrollment increased and workforce participation decreased. This makes our argument more persuasive by showing a consistent pattern. This worked well in emphasizing the contrast, but it could mislead viewers into believing the same trend occurs everywhere. We considered using a broader selection of countries, but decided to focus on these five and added a subtitle explaining that they were intentionally chosen.
  • Using Green and Red to Represent Opposing Trends (Score: +1, Somewhat Earnest). We used green for increases in college enrollment and red for decreases in workforce participation to reinforce the contrast between educational progress and workforce participation. This worked well because it encourages viewers to interpret education increases as positive and workforce declines as negative. We considered using more neutral colors, but chose red and green because they made the visualization more persuasive while keeping the actual values unchanged.
  • Focusing on Women's Changes Rather Than Comparing Men (Score: −2, Deceptive). We chose to show only women's college enrollment and workforce participation to emphasize the contrast between increasing education and declining workforce participation. This made our argument clearer and more persuasive, but excluding men's data could make viewers believe that declining workforce participation is an issue unique to women. We considered including men's workforce participation and found that it also declined in all five countries and regions. We ultimately kept our original comparison to focus on women's educational progress, but recognize that excluding men's data could give viewers an incomplete picture of gender inequality.
  • Highlighting China with an Annotation (Score: +2, Earnest). We added an annotation highlighting China because it had the largest increase in female college enrollment and the largest decrease in female workforce participation among our five selected categories. This persuades viewers by drawing attention to a clear example of the contrast between education and workforce participation. The annotation worked well because it made the takeaway easier to notice without altering the data, although it gives China more attention than the other categories. We considered relying only on the numerical labels but added the annotation to help viewers quickly identify an important observation.
  • Using a Diverging Bar Chart (Score: +1, Somewhat Earnest). We used a diverging bar chart to make the opposing trends immediately noticeable. This worked well because viewers can easily compare the overall changes across countries and regions, but it only shows the difference between the starting and ending years rather than how the values changed over time. We considered using line graphs to show the trends throughout the years, but chose diverging bars because they made the contrast between education and workforce participation more visually persuasive.

Final Reflection

What surprised us the most was that we never had to change a single number in either chart. In the college chart, doing small things to the design, like starting in 1990 or making the women's line purple and bold, was enough to back up our claim. In the workforce chart, using a diverging bar chart, contrasting colors, and an annotation on China helped us emphasize the difference between women's educational progress and their workforce participation. We were honestly surprised by how persuasive, and how misleading, a chart can be when all the data in it is real.

The hardest part was the "against" side. Our first version at the checkpoint was a line chart that mostly relied on a cut-off y-axis, and after looking at it again it felt too obvious, so we switched to the bar chart. To make that one work, we had to leave out places where women's workforce participation actually went up, like Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Latin America, and the chart still looked completely honest because every number was real. We also found out men's workforce participation dropped in all five places we picked, and that some of the drop for women is just young women staying in school longer. Neither of those is on the chart. Picking data like that didn't feel like lying while we were doing it, which is kind of what made it uncomfortable.

We think persuasion is acceptable when we intentionally emphasize certain patterns to make an argument, as long as we stay transparent about how we select, filter, and present our data, and someone looking carefully could still find the full story. For example, our college chart still shows men's enrollment rising even though it isn't emphasized. We would draw the line at manipulating data, hiding important context, or presenting selected trends as universal findings, since those choices mislead viewers instead of just persuading them. That's why we put a note in the subtitle of the workforce chart saying the five places were picked on purpose.
